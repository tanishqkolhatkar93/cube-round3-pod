# Customer Letter

**Written from the perspective of a mid-sized prep center owner**
August 2026 · Internal

> **This is a synthesised voice, not a real customer.** It is a hypothesis about what our target customer would say, assembled from published industry conditions and our own reasoning. It is an empathy and prioritisation device. It is not evidence of demand, and it must not be cited as such. Its purpose is to be falsified or confirmed in the first ten customer conversations. Where it turns out to be wrong, that is the letter doing its job.

---

## Dana Whitfield, owner, Ridgeline Prep — Greenville, South Carolina

I run a prep center. Twenty-eight seller clients, somewhere around fifty-five thousand units a month through a leased warehouse with eleven people on the floor. I have been doing this six years. I charge between forty cents and a dollar ten a unit depending on the work, and I compete against maybe nine other operations within a day's drive who all charge roughly the same thing and all say roughly the same thing on their website.

January changed my business and I am still not sure whether it changed it for the better.

When Amazon stopped prepping seller inventory, a lot of work landed on people who had been quietly relying on Amazon to bag and label things properly. My phone rang for three weeks. My volume is up about thirty percent this year and I hired two people I did not expect to hire. That part is good.

Here is the part that is not good. Every unit that leaves my building now carries a liability that used to be somebody else's. A bag that is not sealed right, a label placed on a seam, a suffocation warning that got covered by the fold, an expiration date that is no longer visible after we shrink-wrapped it — any of those comes back as a fee. Not to me. To my client. Six weeks later.

I want to describe how that conversation goes, because it is the single worst part of my job now.

A client emails me. Amazon charged them a few hundred dollars in unplanned prep fees across a shipment of eight hundred units. They want to know what happened. And I do not know. I genuinely do not know. Those units left here in April. The person who prepped them may not work here anymore. I have a work order that says what we were supposed to do and I have my word that we did it. My client has a fee from Amazon and a spreadsheet. There is no version of that conversation where I come out ahead. Either I eat the fee to keep the account, which I do more often than I would like to admit, or I tell a client I have nothing to show them and watch them start shopping.

I lost two accounts last year that way. Not because we did bad work. Because I could not prove we did good work.

And here is what nobody outside this industry seems to understand: sometimes Amazon is wrong. I am fairly confident a meaningful share of those fees are for defects that did not exist when the unit left my dock, or that got introduced downstream, or that were just miscategorised on receipt. But I cannot contest a single one of them, because contesting requires evidence and I have none. So I absorb charges I did not earn, and my client absorbs the rest, and Amazon has no reason to be more careful.

The other thing I would fix if I could is consistency. I train people well. But eleven people prepping fifty-five thousand units a month will not do it identically, and the variation shows up in exactly the places that cost money. New hire, week three, puts the label somewhere technically flat but close to a seam. Nobody catches it. It scans fine here. It does not scan there. I only find out through a fee.

I also could not tell you today which of my clients' products cause us the most trouble. I know anecdotally — there is a client with a liquid supplement line that is nothing but problems — but I do not have it in a form I could show anyone, including that client, whose products genuinely need different handling than they are paying for.

You asked me about returns, so let me be straight with you.

I do handle returns. Eleven of my twenty-eight clients pay me to process them, and it is honestly some of the least satisfying work in the building. Someone opens a box, looks at a shoe, and decides whether it can go back on the shelf. My people are guessing. They are not trained appraisers and I am not paying appraiser wages. I am fairly sure we throw away things we could sell and re-shelve things we should not, and I could not tell you the ratio because nobody is measuring it.

But if you came to me tomorrow and said you had built something to grade returns better, I would probably tell you it sounds interesting and to call me in the autumn. Returns are maybe fifteen percent of my revenue and none of my anxiety. Prep is a hundred percent of my anxiety.

If you came to me and said you had built something that photographs every unit we prep and gives me a record I can put in front of a client — or in front of Amazon — I would take that call this week. I would want to know what it costs per unit, because at forty cents a unit I do not have room for much. I would want to know it does not slow my line down, because my people are measured on throughput and anything that makes them wait will get worked around within a day. And I would want to know that my clients only see their own units, because two of them compete with each other and would both leave if that got confused.

What I actually want is fairly simple to say and apparently hard to buy.

I want to be able to answer the question "what did you do to my units" with something other than my word. I want to stop paying for defects I did not cause. I want my eleven people to make the same decision as each other on a Friday afternoon. I want to know which products are costing me money so I can price them properly. And frankly, I want something to put on my website that the other nine operations within a day's drive cannot say — because right now the only thing I can compete on is price, and that is a race I am losing slowly.

If your thing does those, I will find the money. If it is one more dashboard I have to log into, I will not.

## What this letter is telling us

The letter was written to be useful, which means it was written to be uncomfortable. Three things it surfaces:

**It does not want the product we are planning to lead with.** Written honestly from a prep-center owner's position, returns grading is a nice-to-have and prep verification is the thing they would take a call about this week. This is the sequencing tension from the 6-Pager, page 5, restated in the customer's own voice — and here it resolves against our plan rather than for it.

That does not automatically mean lead with prep, because prep has materially worse economics: it touches every unit, so it must be metered, and this customer just told us they have forty cents a unit to work with. It means the tension is real and cannot be resolved by argument. It has to be resolved by asking ten of these people, and the question to ask is not "would you buy returns grading" but "rank these three problems."

**The primary value is evidence, not accuracy.** Note what Dana asks for. Not better grading — proof. The line worth building the product around is being able to answer "what did you do to my units" with something other than her word. We had evidence as a feature of the grading product. This letter suggests it is the product and grading is a feature of it.

**Three objections would kill the sale, and all three are already requirements.** Cost per unit against a forty-cent margin, anything that slows the line, and client data separation where two clients compete. Those map to the inference cost budget, fail-open behaviour, and multi-tenant isolation. That they emerge unprompted from the customer's side is mild confirmation the requirements are aimed correctly.

**What to test in the first ten conversations.** Rank prep, returns and pack by urgency. Establish what a fee dispute actually costs them per year, in absorbed charges and lost accounts. Find out what they would pay per unit, and whether it is per unit or a subscription. Ask whether they would pay at all for something they cannot show a client, or whether the client-facing record is the whole value.
